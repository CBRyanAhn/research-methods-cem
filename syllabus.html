How the course works

Learning outcomes

By the end of the course, you should be able to:

  1. Situate your own research interest within the 70-year trajectory of the CEM field and explain why your question is legitimate research now.
  2. Construct and defend a complete horseshoe for your own study — from an observed problem in practice through to a claimed contribution to knowledge and its practical significance.
  3. Diagnose a research problem and state a question that is answerable, non-trivial, and matched to an appropriate design (methodological fit).
  4. Reverse-engineer any empirical CEM paper: identify its claim, its inferential logic, its threats to validity, and what would falsify it.
  5. Execute small-scale instances of six different methods (review, coding, survey, modeling, experiment design, reproduction).
  6. Write and defend a proposal that would survive a real review panel, and write a competent referee report on someone else’s.
  7. Apply research ethics and IRB requirements to your own proposed study.

Course mechanics

4.1 Weekly roles (rotating)

Roles run in W4, W5, W6, W7 and W9–W14 (ten weeks). W1–W3 are framing weeks with their own formats (§5); W8 and W15 are presentation/defense days with no role rotation.

Ten weeks × 7 slots = 70 slots for 16 students, so you’ll get roughly four turns: each of the four roles at least once, with any fifth turn falling in the week closest to your own topic. A three-choice preference list is collected in Week 1 and the fixed roster is published in Week 3, alongside the horseshoe assignment. One backup per week is designated so an absence doesn’t collapse the session.

Role # per week Time Deliverable
Method Lead 2 20 min Explain the logic of the method — assumptions, decision points, what it can and cannot license. Submit a 1-page Method Card, whose final line must answer: which box of the horseshoe does this method serve?
Exemplar Analyst 2 15 min Reverse-engineer a CEM exemplar onto one slide: RQ → design → data → analysis → claim → limits.
Skeptic 2 10 min Attack the exemplar’s validity. Submit a 1-page referee report on the ASCE reviewer form. Repeating the authors’ own stated limitations scores low.
Discussant / Scribe 1 — Chair the open discussion, then post a 300-word synthesis within 48 h.
Everyone else 9 — Entry ticket: one substantive question posted 24 h before class. Two are chosen to open discussion.

4.2 Standard 150-minute running order

Time Segment
0:00–0:15 Instructor framing: where this method sits in the landscape, and the one thing people get wrong
0:15–0:35 Method Leads
0:35–0:50 Exemplar Analysts
0:50–1:00 Skeptics
1:00–1:25 Open discussion from entry tickets (Discussant chairs)
1:25–1:35 Break
1:35–2:15 Method Lab — hands-on, in pairs, laptops required
2:15–2:30 Instructor synthesis + explicit link back to your horseshoe

(Week 5 runs 10–15 minutes longer to absorb the IRB briefing — see §4.6.)

4.3 If the course meets 2 × 75 min

Session A = framing + Method Leads + Exemplar Analysts + Skeptics. Session B = discussion + lab + synthesis. Labs then need to be sized to 45 minutes.

4.4 The Horseshoe Wall

Your horseshoe, built in W3, is posted on the course site and revised weekly. After each method week, update the Research Tasks box if that week gave you a better instrument, or the Theoretical Limitation box if it gave you a better argument. The W7 pre-proposal is a prose expansion of your wall entry.

4.5 Tools

R/RStudio or Python; Zotero; Taguette or NVivo/ATLAS.ti; VOSviewer; G*Power or the pwr package; lavaan and seminr (or SmartPLS); Simphony/AnyLogic or SimPy; OSF for preregistration. All lab datasets are provided; you don’t need to collect your own data for a lab.

4.6 How research ethics is handled

Research ethics doesn’t map well onto the Method Lead / Exemplar Analyst / Skeptic format — there’s no single paper to reverse-engineer the way there is for a method, and the useful skill (writing a correct protocol) benefits from direct instruction more than adversarial debate. It’s handled in three small, well-timed touchpoints instead:

  1. Pre-course module (assigned Week 1, due before Week 4). Read the Belmont Report (12 pp.) and Munafò et al. (2017), “A manifesto for reproducible science,” and review the SNU IRB submission process. No seminar time is spent; completion is checked with a two-question reflection submitted online (credit/no-credit).
  2. A 15-minute briefing in Week 4’s framing segment. What a defensible protocol looks like for construction research specifically — site access, vulnerable populations (migrant labor, as in the Tutt et al. reading), and physical risk for sensor/XR studies. You leave with the assignment: draft the IRB protocol section for your own horseshoe study, due at the start of Week 5.
  3. A 10-minute Q&A at the open of Week 5, before the qualitative-interviewing content begins, addressing common problems in the submitted drafts. The drafts themselves aren’t separately graded as a lab — they feed directly into the “Ethics, feasibility, and timeline” line of the final proposal rubric (§7.3).

If your program’s IRB office requires a certified training module (e.g., CITI training) before you can submit protocols, that sits alongside item 1 above.

Assessment

Component Weight Notes
Attendance, entry tickets, and pre-course ethics module 10% Credit/no credit; 2 free passes
Seminar roles 20% ~4 turns per student; rubric in §7.1
Horseshoe assignments (W3 reconstruction + own horseshoe, maintained on the wall) 10% Graded twice: W3 and W11
Method Labs (6 graded deliverables) 15% Labs 1, 2, 4, 5, 6, 9 below; 2–3 pages each, due one week after
Peer review assignment 10% Two referee reports on classmates’ pre-proposals
Pre-proposal (2 pages) + presentation 10% Due W7, presented W8
Final proposal (8–10 pages) + panel defense 25% Due end of W14, defended W15

Grade-on-a-curve, A–F. Absence over 1/3 of sessions results in F/U except where the instructor deems the cause unavoidable.

Note on generative AI. You may use LLMs for language editing, code scaffolding, and literature triage, and must disclose usage in a short appendix to each written deliverable. LLM output may not be submitted as the analytic contribution — an LLM may not be the sole coder in the qualitative lab. Weeks 5 and 13 treat LLM-assisted analysis as a methodological question in its own right, which is the productive way to handle this.

7.2 Horseshoe rubric (0–20, applied in W3 and W11)

Criterion Pts
Observed problem is specific enough that success criteria are visible 4
Theoretical limitation is grounded in literature, not asserted 4
Research questions are testable, with metrics named 4
Research tasks include a defensible test method (Fischer’s four, or an alternative justified) 4
Contribution to knowledge and practical significance are distinguishable from each other 4

7.3 Proposal rubric (100 pts)

Criterion Pts
Problem significance and framing 15
Question: answerable, specific, non-trivial 15
Methodological fit — design justified against alternatives 25
Data, sampling, and analysis plan feasible at MS scale 15
Threats to validity identified and addressed 15
Ethics, feasibility, and timeline (includes the IRB protocol drafted in Week 4–5) 5
Writing and reference quality 10

7.4 Referee report rubric (per report, 0–10)

Specificity of critique (4), constructiveness and actionability (3), correct application of methodological standards (2), professional tone (1).
