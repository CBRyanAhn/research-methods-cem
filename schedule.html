Routledge. § on falsifiability (excerpt). - Seymour, D., & Rooke, J. (1995). “The culture of the industry and the culture of research.” CME, 13(6), 511–523.

→ If you only have time for one: Creswell & Creswell, Ch. 1–3. It sets the vocabulary (paradigm, design, method) that every later week assumes you already have.

In-class activity: The “Poang chair” exercise — classify ten candidate statements as science / engineering / consulting, then state for each what evidence would count as disconfirmation. Ends with each student writing a one-sentence research interest on a card; the cards return in Week 15.

Admin: submit your three-choice role preferences; you’ll be assigned one published CEM paper to bring to Week 3; the pre-course ethics module is assigned today, due before Week 4.

Week 2 (9/10) — History of CEM research

Focus: How the field’s standards of evidence were built, and why the current track list looks the way it does

Instructor segment (25 min): the four-era arc — 1955–75 (emergence: Peurifoy, Fondahl’s precedence diagramming, Oglesby & Parker’s operations analysis, Gates/Carr bidding debate, Halpin’s CYCLONE), 1975–95 (institutionalization: JCEM, CRC, CII, CIFE; Ashley on risk sharing; Tatum on innovation; Howell/Tommelein/Ballard on lean; Levitt on organizational design; expert systems, neural nets, GAs), 1995–2005 (Peña-Mora on collaboration technology; sensors, RFID, GPS; Fischer’s 4D CAD; Martinez & Kamat simulation; Hendrickson & Horvath EIO-LCA), 2005–present (reality capture, automation, and the turn toward social science).

Student workshop (4 teams × 4): each team takes one era and two landmark works from it. The presentation question is not “what did they find” but “what counted as a legitimate method then, what data existed, and what got it published?” Each team closes by mapping current CRC / i3CE track names onto their era.

Readings - Levitt, R. E. (2007). “CEM research for the next 50 years: Maximizing economic, environmental, and societal value of the built environment.” JCEM, 133(9), 619–628. [Peurifoy Award Lecture] - Taylor, J. E., & Jaselskis, E. J. (2010). “Introduction to the special issue on research methodologies in CEM.” JCEM, 136(1), 1–2. - Jahren, C. T., et al. (2023). “Second special collection on research methodologies in CEM.” JCEM, 149(2). - CRC and i3CE current track listings (2 pp., handout).

→ If you only have time for one: Levitt (2007). It’s short, it’s the field talking about itself, and every team will need it regardless of which era they draw.

Lab (method archaeology): each student is assigned one JCEM volume — half from the 1980s–90s, half from the last three years — and codes its table of contents using the §3 taxonomy. The class assembles a shared method-frequency chart across eras.

Assignment for W3: read Fischer (2006) and reconstruct the horseshoe for the paper assigned in W1.

Week 3 (9/17) — Formalizing construction knowledge: the CIFE horseshoe

Focus: The field’s own research process model — and the framework the rest of this course fills in

Instructor segment (25 min): the seven boxes — Observed Problem in Practice → Theoretical Limitation → Research Questions → Research Tasks → Research Results → Contribution to Knowledge → Practical Significance, with Intuition running underneath. Worked through with the Disney California Adventure lagoon / 4D modeling example (Akbas 2003). Two things Fischer insists on: the problem statement must be specific enough that success criteria are visible, and the maximum anxiety principle — always advance the step with the greatest uncertainty first.

Studio (the core of the week): present the horseshoe you reconstructed for your assigned paper. Pairs then debate one question — which box is that paper weakest at? Most CEM papers collapse the Theoretical Limitation and Contribution to Knowledge boxes and jump from problem straight to tasks.

Fischer’s four test methods, mapped onto this course:

Fischer’s test method Where this course teaches it
Variation studies on retrospective cases W10 (archival identification), W12 (simulation experiments)
Expert panel review W7 (Delphi and structured elicitation)
Charrette test W10 (controlled experiment design), W11 (XR-enabled charrettes)
Prospective / intervention case study W6 (case study design), W10 (multiple-baseline field designs), W14 (action research)

Readings - Fischer, M. (2006). “Formalizing construction knowledge for concurrent performance-based design.” Proc. 13th EG-ICE Workshop, Ascona, Switzerland. (Springer LNCS 4200.) - Van de Ven, A. H. (1989). “Nothing is quite so practical as a good theory.” AMR, 14(4), 486–489. - Clayton, M. J., Kunz, J. C., & Fischer, M. A. (1998). The Charrette Test Method. CIFE Technical Report #120, Stanford University. - Hevner, A. R., March, S. T., Park, J., & Ram, S. (2004). “Design science in information systems research.” MIS Quarterly, 28(1), 75–105. §1–2 only — for comparison in W14. - Reference: Akbas, R. (2003). Geometry Based Modeling and Simulation of Construction Processes. PhD thesis, Stanford University.

→ If you only have time for one: Fischer (2006) itself. Everything else this week supports it.

⚠️ A limit worth knowing now. The horseshoe grew out of CIFE/VDC work and fits artifact-producing research beautifully. It fits interpretive and purely descriptive research much less well — if you’re running an interview study, you’ll struggle to fill Practical Significance honestly. Keep this in mind; Week 6 introduces a contrasting map (Eisenhardt’s inductive process model) for exactly this reason.

A worked local example. Alongside Fischer’s Disney-lagoon case, we’ll trace one research line from this department all the way around the horseshoe: deictic gesture-based spatial referencing for human–robot collaboration. Observed problem (site instructions to robots are improvised and imprecise) → theoretical limitation (gesture-based HRI evaluated only in small, controlled spaces) → question → tasks → results → contribution → practical significance. The published endpoint is Yoon, Kim, Park & Ahn (2023), JCEM, 149(7), which returns as an exemplar in Week 11. Seeing a full horseshoe from a project you could walk down the hall and ask about makes the abstract boxes concrete.

Assignment (ongoing): build the horseshoe for your own research idea. Post it to the Horseshoe Wall — it’s revised weekly from here and becomes the W7 pre-proposal.

Admin: the fixed role roster is published today.

On mixed methods specifically: a clean, single-paper CEM exemplar that is unambiguously “mixed methods done well” is genuinely hard to find — it’s an underused design in this field. Revisit Spearing et al. (2022, Week 5) through a mixed-methods lens: it applies three qualitative techniques to one dataset, which is adjacent to but not the same as integrating qualitative and quantitative strands. Treat the absence of a tidy exemplar here as data about the field, and flag it in your own proposal if you’re the one attempting a genuine mixed-methods design.

Discussion prompt: Lay Eisenhardt’s inductive process model beside Fischer’s horseshoe on the board. Where do they contradict each other, and which of your classmates’ Horseshoe Wall entries would be better served by the other map?

Lab 3 (in-class): Given a research question handed out in class, each pair specifies a case-study design — unit of analysis, case selection logic, number of cases and why, embedded units, triangulation sources, and the specific rival explanation the design rules out. Pairs then attack each other’s designs.

Week 8 (10/22) — Pre-proposal presentations, peer review, and journal fit

No new readings.

  1. Present the pre-proposal in 6 minutes + 4 minutes Q&A — problem, gap, question, proposed design, why that design. The horseshoe is the required slide format.
  2. You’re assigned two peers’ pre-proposals and write a referee report using the ASCE reviewer form and the COPE Ethical Guidelines for Peer Reviewers (2017).
  3. Reports are returned anonymously; write a one-page response-to-reviewers letter (due W10).
  4. Journal fit clinic: name a target journal and justify the fit against its aims, typical methods, and recent volumes.

Closing discussion (the bookend to Week 3). Put Fischer’s horseshoe and Hevner’s DSR framework side by side. A large share of CEM papers propose a framework or system and “validate” it on one case.
